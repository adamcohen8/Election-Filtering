Election Voters
National
October 23 – 24, 2026 | n=1500 | ±2.53%

Insights & Analysis

State of the Midterms
–  The macro political environment hasn’t changed much since earlier this month. Democrats
   lead the generic ballot 51% to 43% (D+8), and every survey since August has landed within a
   point of that.
      –  Women under 55 back Democrats by 29 points (62                -33), and men 55 and over back
         Republicans by 16 (56-40). Women 55 and over lean Democratic by 7, men under 55 by
         11.
–  The Democrat enthusiasm we’ve been warning about is not a polling anomaly. It can be seen
   in the early vote returns across the country and those who already voted in this NVT say they
   backed Democrats by a 2:1 margin (63-35, D+28) while being D+21 on the retro, while those
   yet to vote split 46 -   47 (D+1) and are R+5 on the retro.
      –  The early electorate skews older. Of voters 65 and over, 36% have voted, compared
         with 30% of voters aged 55 to 64, 21% to 23% of voters 30 to 54, and 19% of voters 18
         to 29.
      –  80% of Democrats call themselves extremely motivated to vote, versus 67% of
         Republicans –     a 13-point motivation delta.
            –   Motivation falls to 47% among voters under 30 and 37% among swing voters.
            –   Traditional conservative Republicans sit at 63%, 10 points behind Trump
                Republicans.

Insights & Analysis

      –   Republicans are a little less locked in than Democrats. 77% of Republicans are definitely
          Republican, and 14% are only probably. Democrats are 91% definite. One in 10
          Independents is still unsure.
–  Voters call the country's direction no worse than they did at this point in 2024 and better than
   in 2022, yet the ballot is worse than both years. The difference is Trump.
      –   His net favorable is 12 points lower than in Oct 2024 and 8 lower than in Oct 2022, and
          the committed vote shows it: definite Republicans fell from 41% in 2024 to 34%, while
          definite Democrats rose from 41% to 45%.
–  The GOP base is less settled than early this month. Republicans saying wrong track rose
   from 20% to 26%, and traditional conservative Republicans fell from R+86 to R+76 on the
   generic.
–  Republicans need a big Election Day. Nearly half of Republican voters (48%) plan to vote on
   November 3rd, compared with 29% of Democratic voters. Democrats have already banked
   votes equal to 17% of the electorate, compared to Republicans at just 10%.
      –   Election Day makes up 53% of the votes still to be cast, and Republicans lead those
          voters by +15 points. Every other vote method favors Democrats, from early in-person
          (+11) to mail (+32).
            –   Half of non-college men (48%) and rural voters (51%) plan to vote that day, and
                Republicans carry those groups by 9 and 22 points.

Insights & Analysis

–  Swing voters are back with Republicans. Among voters who haven’t cast a ballot, soft
   Republicans lead soft Democrats R+13, after trailing -2 in early October. That’s a 15-point net
   swing, bigger than September’s R+7 edge.
Issues
–  Republicans have lost ground on five of six trust issues since February. Cost of living moved
   the most, from a 1-point Republican edge to a 12-point Democratic lead (48-36). Immigration
   held at R+12 to R+11.
      –  Soft Republicans’ trust in the GOP fell from 76% to 58%, and men under 55 moved from
         R+2 to D+19.
      –  Voters earning $50K-$100K swung 22 points (R+4 to D+18). Men 55 and over moved
         the least, 8 points.
–  Trust on who will best manage the economy moved from R+3 to D+6. Voters aged 45 to 54
   swung 17 points, and middle-income voters 19.
–  Immigration held at R+11 overall, but swing voters moved toward Republicans, from R+30 to
   R+41. Hispanic voters went the other way, from R+1 to D+11.
–  Crime narrowed from R+13 to R+7, led by voters earning $50K to $100K (R+19 to even),
   Black voters (D+36 to D+54), and Hispanic voters (R+4 to D+11). Swing voters held at R+37.
–  Wasteful spending fell from R+12 to R+4, with voters 65 and over moving from R+20 to R+8.
   Republicans who trust neither party on spending doubled, from 7% to 14%.

Insights & Analysis

–  Foreign affairs moved from R+1 to D+5. Black voters swung 19 points; men 55 and over were
   the only gender-age group to move toward Republicans (R+18 to R+20).
Images
–  Trump’s image among those yet-to-vote improved from -18 in early October to -12 now.
   Swing voters went from -33 to -16.
      –  Women yet-to-vote drive more of the movement here. Women 55 and over went from -
         23 to -4, and women under 55 from -45 to -30.
      –  Men under 55 keep souring (-16 to -19), and non-college men fell from +12 to +0. The
         under-55 gender gap shrank from 29 points to 11.
–  Congressional Republicans improved from -17 to -5 among voters still to vote, and to even
   with swing voters (from -20). Men 55 and over jumped from +10 to +24.
      –  Congressional Democrats slipped from -14 to -15 overall, and fell from -23 to -36 with
         swing voters. Women 55 and over went from -1 to -11.
–  The “voting to oppose Trump” motivation is narrower among voters still to vote, +7, versus
   +10 in the full sample. Men under 55 sit at +13.
–  Mike Johnson sits at 31% to 44% and Hakeem Jeffries at 30% to 41%. More than 40% of
   swing voters can’t rate either.

Insights & Analysis

Campaign Communications Environment
–  Of voters, 85% recall at least one campaign contact in the past two weeks, led by texts (67%)
   and mail (47%). Only 10% recall a door knock.
      –  Early voters recall more of everything: texts (77% to 64%), mail (56% to 44%), and door
         knocks (14% to 8%). Either campaigns chased those votes harder than everyone else
         or engaged voters remember more.
–  Soft Republicans recall less campaign contact than other swing voters. Of soft Republicans,
   53% recall a text, against 67% of soft Democrats and undecideds combined, and 17% recall
   no contact at all, against 10%.
      –  The gaps hold across methods. Door knocks run 7% to 13%, email 23% to 36%, and
         automated calls 20% to 31%.
–  Voters split evenly on which party they recall hearing about (26% each), but they remember
   contact about Democratic campaigns (+13 net) more warmly than contact about Republicans
   (+6).
      –  Women under 55 recall contact about Democrats over Republicans 32% to 22%, and
         voters 18 to 29 recall it 37% to 25%. Men 55 and over recall Republican contact 29% to
         22%.
      –  Swing voters recall Republican contact more (22% to 15%), but 40% remember it as
         mostly negative.

Insights & Analysis

      –  Traditional Republicans recall positive GOP outreach at -20 points below Trump
         Republicans (28% vs. 48%).
–  Cost of living still leads the type of ads voters recall most at 48%, but is down 4 points since
   October 2024. Immigration recall is sliced in half compared to the presidential from 63% to
   31%, and abortion from 54% to 6%. Gas prices (45%) and the Iran war (31%) are new to the
   list.
–  Since early October, voters recall more immigration ads on the Republican side and fewer on
   the Democratic side. Republicans recall immigration ads at 41%, up from 30%; Democrats at
   22%, down from 29%.
      –  Swing voters recall fewer cost-of-living ads (51% to 42%) and more immigration ads
         (24% to 30%), in step with their move toward Republicans on immigration trust.
      –  Housing affordability ad recall doubled among voters under 30 (11% to 25%).
Election Confidence
–  Asked what concerns them most about the upcoming election voters are split with 33%
   picking eligible voters being blocked or intimidated at the polls, 28% picking ineligible people
   casting ballots, and 23% picking ballot tampering during the count.
      –  Fear of blocked voters peaks among Black voters (45%) and voters 18 to 29 (41%).
         Fear of ineligible voters peaks among rural voters (41%) and men 55 and over (39%).
         Swing voters spread across the three, and 17% name none.

Insights & Analysis

–  Committed partisans are the least willing to accept the results as legitimate if their party loses
   Congress. Voters definitely      backing one party would accept 54-31, while swing voters would
   accept 69-14.
      –  Trump Republicans (38%), non-college women (33%), and Black voters (33%) reject a
         loss at the highest rates. College men reject at the lowest rate, 21%.
      –  Voters who expect a definite win are the most likely to reject a loss: 42% of those certain
         Republicans win, and 38% of those certain Democrats win.
–  Asked when they expect to know which party won Congress, two-thirds (66%) say by the day
   after the election: 32% expect it on election night and 34% the next day. Only 9% expect it to
   take more than a week.
      –  Republicans expect a faster result. Of Republicans, 40% expect a winner on election
         night vs 29% of Democrats. That rises to 51% among voters certain Republicans will
         win, and 43% among voters most worried about ineligible people voting.
      –  Western voters expect the slowest count, with 23% expecting a result on election night
         and 13% expecting it to take more than a week. Voters under 30 also lean slow, with
         15% expecting more than a week.
After the Election
–  If Democrats win the House, voters want them to start with prices. Asked to pick two
   priorities, 35% choose lowering gas and energy prices, 31% ending the Iran war, and 23%
   working with Trump on shared priorities. Investigations (20%) and impeachment (18%) rank
   lower.

Insights & Analysis

      –   Among Democrats, 46% pick ending the Iran war, 35% investigations, 32%
          impeachment, and 26% gas prices. Traditional Democrats back impeachment (32%) as
          much as progressives (33%).
      –   Gas prices top the list for swing voters (51%), rural voters (42%), and moderates (41%).
          Only 10% of swing voters pick investigations, and 10% pick impeachment.
–  If Republicans lose Congress, Trump takes the most blame (35%), followed by high prices
   and the cost of living (20%) and voter fraud or unfair elections (10%).
      –   College-educated Republicans spread the blame across Trump (19%), voter fraud
          (18%), and prices (17%). Non-college Republicans point to fraud (25%), with only 11%
          naming Trump.
–  10% of Trump voters aren’t with the Republican on the generic ballot (6% to the Dem and 4%
   to undecided). These voters 51% want Democrats to lower gas and energy prices and 38%
   to end the Iran war.
      –   Only 17% choose investigations and 8% impeachment. That is less than half the rate of
          Democrats overall (35% and 32%).
      –   If the GOP loses, these lost Trump voters blame the President as the top blame target
          (37%), prices are next (21%), and the Iran war is third (18%).
–  Nearly half of voters (47%) say Trump should have little or no influence over the party after a
   loss, and 37% say none at all. Another 30% want him to keep a ton or a great deal of
   influence, including 54% of Republicans.

Insights & Analysis

      –  Age is a big divide inside the GOP with younger Republicans wanting less Trump
         influence after a loss. Of Republicans under 45, 41% want him to keep heavy influence,
         compared to 54% of those 45-to-64 and 64% of those 65+.
–  On where the party should go once Trump leaves office, voters divide into three nearly equal
   camps: a third (32%) want to keep Trump’s policies, with or without his style, 31% want a
   traditional conservative direction, and 29% want the party to move to the center.
      –  Republicans want to keep the Trump policies: 35% would continue Trump’s policies and
         style, and 30% would keep the policies with a different style. Another 29% want to
         change course, 19% toward traditional conservatism and 10% toward the center.
Latent Class Analysis Voter Segments
–  Trump Loyalists (23%): Hardcore Republicans (95%) who view Trump very favorably (78%)
   and rank immigration first (41%). They vote reliably: 79% are extremely motivated, and 55%
   voted in all four recent general elections.
      –  Older, white, and rural. Of this segment, 65% are 55 or over, 83% are white, 51% are
         white without a college degree, and 26% are rural. Another 23% live in the South, and
         65% are married. College women make up only 15%.
      –  They plan to vote on Election Day (46%) and get news from cable (40%) and talk radio
         (14%) over national broadcast (15%). If Republicans lose, 38% would reject the result.
–  Policy-First Republicans (18%): Republicans who vote on policy more than loyalty to
   Trump. They’re 63% hard and 34% soft Republican, 59% vote on Republican ideals, and
   36% call themselves Independents. Half (50%) are extremely motivated.

Insights & Analysis

      –  Affluent married parents. Of this segment, 13% earn $250K or more (8% overall), 67%
         are married, and 35% have children at home. Their gender and age mix matches the
         electorate, and 31% call themselves moderate.
      –  They pay the least attention to political news: 16% follow none (9% overall). Of this
         segment, 82% haven’t voted, 51% plan to vote on Election Day, and 51% want a
         Democratic House to work with Trump.
–  Frustrated Middle (14%): Disaffected voters who lean Democratic, 50% to 15%, with 35%
   undecided. In 2024 they split 34% Harris, 32% Trump, and 24% third party or no vote. Of this
   segment, 82% say wrong track and 49% name inflation as their top priority.
      –  Younger men in small towns. Men under 55 make up 33%, voters under 30 17%, small                        -
         town voters 29%, singles 30%, and voters earning under $25K 13%. Only 39% voted in
         all four elections, against 52% overall.
      –  You reach them through national broadcast news (35%) and TikTok (15%) more than
         cable (25%). They plan to vote on Election Day (43%), and 13% would reject a loss, the
         lowest of any segment.
–  Democratic Coalition (44%): Hard Democrats (94%), 80% extremely motivated, who drive
   the early vote: 35% have already voted, 19% by mail. Threats to democracy rank first for
   29%, and 51% call themselves progressive.
      –  Female, college-educated, and diverse. Women make up 62% and college women 32%.
         Black voters are 19%, Hispanic voters 14%, urban voters 25%, and singles 30%. Only
         24% are white without a degree.

Insights & Analysis

–  They follow news most closely: 34% pay a great deal of attention, through national broadcast
   (39%), news websites (35%), and newspapers (16%). They want the House to end the Iran
   war (47%) and investigate Trump (35%).
