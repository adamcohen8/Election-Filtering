About this research

This Pew Research Center analysis examines Americans’ engagement with the upcoming
midterm elections, views of the state of the country, and attitudes about the political parties and
Donald Trump’s presidency.

Why did we do this?
Pew Research Center conducts research to help the public, media and decision-makers
understand important topics. We have studied Americans’ views of politics and major policy
issues for decades.

Learn more about Pew Research Center.

How did we do this?
We surveyed 5,062 U.S. adults, including 3,917 registered voters, from Sept. 28 to Oct. 4, 2026.
Everyone who took part in this survey is a member of the Center’s American Trends Panel. The
survey represents the views of the full U.S. adult population.

Here are the survey questions used for this analysis, the detailed responses and the survey
methodology.

With Americans in a Sour Mood, Democratic
Voters Are More Engaged in Midterms
Trump approval drops to 30%, as 44% of voters see their vote as
‘against’ him

The 2026 midterm election is taking place amid deep public dissatisfaction with the state of the
nation, near-record low approval ratings of President Donald Trump, and growing negativity
about national economic conditions and the ongoing conflict with Iran.

Against this backdrop, Democratic candidates now hold an 8 percentage point edge over
Republican candidates in preferences for the House of Representatives among all registered
voters nationally.

And while many registered
voters will not turn out to vote
this fall, Democratic
candidates are also
buoyed by their
supporters’ relatively high
levels of engagement and
stated motivation to vote
when compared with
those who are backing
Republican candidates.
For example:

▪   78% of voters who back
    their House district’s
    Democratic candidate say
    it “really matters” which
    party wins control of
    Congress – 9 points higher
    than the share of Republican backers who say this.
▪   In contrast, Republican candidate supporters were 4 points higher on this measure than
    Democratic supporters at a similar point before the 2022 midterm election.

The partisan gap is also wide on other measures of engagement: Democratic supporters are 14
points more likely than Republican supporters to say they have given this year’s election “a lot”
of thought and 17 points more likely to say they are “extremely mo          tivated” to vote this year.

A new Pew Research Center survey of 5,062 U.S. adults – including 3,917 registered voters –
conducted Sept. 28-Oct. 4, 2026, also finds that Donald Trump’s approval rating now stands at
30% – the lowest of his second term. Most voters (63%) consider Trump a factor in their vote for
Congress, with far more thinking of their choice as a vote against the president (44%) than for
him (19%).

As economic ratings turn more negative and concerns about prices rise – particularly for gas
and energy – the economy continues to rank as the top voting issue this year, across
the political spectrum: 84% of registered voters – including 88% of those who support
Democrats and 82% who back Republicans – say it is very important to their vote.

The midterm race

Democrats currently hold an 8 percentage
point edge in national vote preferences, with
44% of all registered voters saying they would
support a Democrat for the House if the
election were held today and 36% preferring a
Republican. Another 5% say they would select
a different candidate and 15% say they aren’t
sure.

Among validated voters – confirmed to
have voted in the 2024 election – those
who voted for Trump are less
consolidated behind GOP candidates
(77%) than Kamala Harris’ voters are
behind Democratic candidates (87%).

The difference is even more pronounced
among voters who are disaffected with the
party they backed in 2024 – who make up a
quarter of each 2024 coalition:

▪   49% of Trump voters who have an
    unfavorable view of the GOP today support Republican candidates for the House this year.
    While only 12% say they back Democrats, more than a third say either that they are “not
    sure” (25%) or prefer “another candidate” (14%).

▪   In contrast, Harris voters with a negative view of the Democratic Party are much more solid
    in their preferences for Democrats this year: 73% say they support Democratic House
    candidates.

In both 2024 coalitions, these disaffected voters are less engaged with the election than those
who have positive views of the parties – but that is particularly the case among Trump’s 2024
voters. For instance, just 36% of Trump voters with an unfavorable view of the GOP say the
outcome this November really matters. By comparison, 65% of Harris voters with a negative
view of the Democratic Party say it does.

Other key findings

Satisfaction with the state of the nation has dropped to 39% among Republicans
overall (including those who lean to the GOP). This is the lowest of Trump’s second term
(down from 65% shortly after he entered office and 54% at the beginning of this year). Overall,
22% of Americans say they are satisfied with the state of the nation, while 77% are dissatisfied.

Majorities of voters consider the future of democracy (73%), healthcare (68%),
education (62%), immigration (59%) and foreign policy (57%) as important to their
vote. As in past elections, immigration is a more central issue for Republican voters (72% say
it’s very important) than for Democratic voters (53%) – but Democratic voters see it as a more
important issue than they did four years ago.

The U.S. military action in Iran remains
deeply unpopular, and most Americans
think it won’t end soon. A third of
Americans approve of how Trump is handling
the conflict – down from 37% in March,
shortly after it began. About three-quarters of
Americans (77%) expect the conflict to last
another six months or more.

Most voters (61%) say they dislike
elected officials who support building
data centers in their area; just 8% like
this. While dislike is especially widespread
among Democratic voters (75% dislike), on
balance Republican voters also do not like leaders who support data centers (44% dislike, 14%
like).

Midterm Vote Preferences by Race and Ethnicity, Age and
Other Demographics

This is part of a larger report on
the attitudes and dynamics
shaping the 2026 midterm
elections.

With several weeks to go until
Election Day, Americans who are
registered to vote favor
Democrats over Republicans in
the national race for the House of
Representatives by an 8
percentage point margin (44% to
36%). About two-in-ten
registered voters say they would
vote for a third-party candidate
or are not sure.

This analysis is among all
registered voters – many of
whom will not turn out in this
November’s midterm elections.
For example, 46% of those
eligible voted in the 2022
midterm elections.

While those who turn out in
elections often differ from
those who do not, including
in their vote choice, the ways
groups of voters overall view the
choice illustrate wide – and
familiar – differences in voting
preferences by gender, race,
education, age and political
party.

Gender

Men are evenly divided: 42% say they would vote for the Democratic candidate in their district,
and 42% say they would vote for the Republican.

Women support Democratic candidates by a 14-point margin, 45% vs. 31% for Republicans.

Race and ethnicity

White voters tilt toward Republicans, 44% to 37%.

Among Hispanic voters, Democratic candidates hold a 51% to 24% advantage. A quarter of
Hispanic voters say they would support another candidate or are not sure.

In the 2024 presidential election, Hispanic voters were nearly evenly split in their vote
preference. But in previous midterm and presidential elections over the past two decades, they
have supported Democrats by considerable margins.

Seven-in-ten Black voters support Democratic candidates, while 7% back Republicans.

Among Asian voters, 56% support Democrats, and 16% choose Republicans. A combined 27%
would vote for another candidate or are not sure.

Age

As in previous midterm elections, younger voters tilt Democratic. Voters ages 50 and older are
more divided:

▪   Voters ages 18 to 29 prefer Democrats over Republicans by a two                   -to-one margin: 51% to
    23%. A quarter say they are not sure or would support another candidate.
▪   Voters 30 to 49 tilt toward Democrats – 43% vs. 31% for Republicans – with 26% backing
    another candidate or saying they’re not sure.
▪   Republicans have a modest advantage among voters 50 to 64, 45% to 39%.
▪   Voters 65 and older are divided: 44% support Democrats, and 41% back Republicans.

Education

Like in recent elections, voters with bachelor’s degrees or more education are more likely than
those with less education to support Democratic candidates.





                                                www.pewresearch.org

                                                        10
                                             PEW RESEARCH CENTER

Democrats have a wide advantage among voters with a postgraduate degree (58% vs.
Republicans’ 30%). They also have a sizable, though narrower, advantage among voters with a
bachelor’s degree but without a postgraduate degree (48% to 33%).

Voters who attended some college but do not have bachelor’s degrees (42% for
Democrats and 38% for Republicans) and voters who did not attend college (35% for
Democrats and 39% for Republicans) are more divided.

Party identification

Though very few voters who identify with or lean toward a political party say they would vote for
the other party’s congressional candidates, Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents
are more united than their GOP counterparts.

Among Republicans and Republican leaners, 73% prefer Republican candidates, while 6%
prefer Democrats. About two-in-ten would pick another candidate or are not sure.

By contrast, 82% of Democrats and Democratic leaners say they would vote for
Democratic candidates, and just 2% would vote for Republicans. About 15% prefer another
candidate or are not sure.

For more information on groups’ congressional vote preferences, refer to the detailed tables.





 A specific, not generic, ballot

 For this survey, respondents were presented with the names and partisan affiliations of the
 major-party candidates on the ballot in their congressional district, based on geographic
 information they provided. They were also presented with options to choose “another
 candidate” or say they were not sure.

 Respondents in Alaska (where there are four candidates on the general election ballot),
 Louisiana and Missouri (where a late change to the congressional map has resulted in an
 open, majority-vote primary held in November) received generic Republican and
 Democratic candidate choices. In districts where one of the two major parties did not field a
 candidate, respondents were given the option to choose a generic member of the party
 opposite the named candidate in their district.

Midterm preferences and engagement

Voters who are highly attentive to this year’s elections favor Democrats by a wide margin.

The Democratic advantage is not as wide among all those who turned out to vote in either the
most recent presidential (2024) or midterm (2022) elections.

Economy Ranks as Top Issue in Midterms for All Voters –
But Partisans Differ Over the Importance of Other Issues

The economy is the dominant
issue for midterm voters in
this fall’s congressional
elections.

As Americans express growing
concerns about economic
conditions and the cost of
necessities, 84% of registered
voters say the economy is very
important to their midterm
vote.

It is the top issue among both
voters who prefer Republican
candidates in the
congressional elections (82%)
and those who back
Democrats (88%).

Clear majorities of Republican
and Democratic voters also
say the future of democracy is
very important to their vote –
though Democrats are more
likely to say this than
Republicans (85% vs. 69%,
respectively).

on the attitudes and dynamics
shaping the 2026 midterm

Republican and Democratic voters differ considerably over the importance of
many other issues asked about on the survey.

The two largest differences across the 16 items asked on the survey are on “investigations into
Trump and his administration” (56 percentage point gap), as well as environmental and climate
policy (51 percentage point gap) – both of which rank as very important for most Democratic
voters but far fewer Republican voters.

There are also sizable gaps on violent crime (which Republican voters are considerably more
likely to see as important to their vote) and issues around race and ethnicity, healthcare and
education (which Democrats are more likely to see as important to their vote).

Artificial intelligence and the 2026 midterms

Artificial intelligence (AI) ranks fairly low as an election issue: 44% of voters say it
is very important to their vote this year. However, Democratic voters (54%) are more
likely to say it is very important to their vote than Republican voters (34%).

in their area.

How do 2026 midterm voting issues compare to 2022?
The economy is the top voting issue for both Republican and Democratic voters in 2026. But
Republican voters are less likely to say it is very important to their vote than they were to say
this at a similar point before the 2022 midterms (82% now vs. 92% then). The reverse is true
among Democratic voters: The economy’s importance has risen 23 percentage points since 2022
(88% today vs. 65% then).

Immigration is seen as a more important voting issue among Democratic voters today than four
years ago (+17 percentage points). Among Republican voters, 72% say it is important today and
76% said it was important in 2022.

Abortion and gun policy are also considerably less central to Democratic voters’ considerations
this year than in 2022. Among Republican voters, education policy and energy policy are less
important than they were four years ago.

Trump’s Approval Rating Drops as Fewer Americans Are
Confident in His Handling of the Economy, Other Issues
31% of Trump’s 2024               voters      disapprove of           his job performance

Three-in-ten Americans now approve of the way Donald Trump is handling his job as president,
while 68% disapprove.

Trump’s approval rating has dropped substantially over the course of his second term – his
current rating is comparable to the lowest rating of his first term (29% according to a Pew
Research Center survey conducted in the days between Jan. 6, 2021, and the end of his first
term).

Confidence in Trump’s handling of several key issues, including the economy, foreign policy and
immigration, is also lower than at the beginning of his term. Also, most Americans (69%) say
they have little confidence in Trump to keep his business interests separate from his presidential
decision-making.

This is part of a larger report on the attitudes and dynamics shaping the 2026 midterm
elections.
Trump’s approval among his 2024 voters
The decline in Trump’s approval ratings is particularly pronounced among those who voted for
him in 2024, and among those who did not vote:

▪   67% of Trump’s 2024 voters now approve of his performance, down from 75% in July, 83%
    at the beginning of this year and 95% early in his second term.
▪   Today, 24% of those who did not vote in 2024 approve of Trump’s performance.

Methodology
The American Trends Panel                      survey     methodology

Overview
Data in this report comes from Wave 200 of the American Trends Panel (ATP), Pew Research
Center’s nationally representative panel of randomly selected U.S. adults. The survey was
conducted from Sept. 28 to Oct. 4, 2026. A total of 5,062 panelists responded out of 5,835 who
were sampled, for a survey-level response rate of 87%.

The cumulative response rate accounting for nonresponse to the recruitment surveys and
attrition is 3%. The break-off rate among panelists who logged on to the survey and completed at
least one item is 1%. The margin of sampling error for the full sample of 5,062 respondents is
plus or minus 1.6 percentage points.

The survey includes oversamples of Hispanic adults, non-Hispanic Asian adults and 18- to 29-
year-olds in order to provide more precise estimates of the opinions and experiences                     of these
smaller demographic subgroups.           These oversampled groups are weighted back to reflect their
correct proportions in the population.

SSRS conducted the survey for Pew Research Center via online (n=4,874) and live telephone
(n=188) interviewing. Interviews were conducted in both English and Spanish.

To learn more about the ATP, read “         About the American Trends Panel            .”

Panel recruitment
Since 2018, the ATP has used address-based sampling (ABS) for recruitment. A study cover
letter and a pre-incentive are mailed to a stratified, random sample of households selected from
the U.S. Postal Service’s Computerized Delivery Sequence File. This Postal Service file has been
estimated to cover 90% to 98% of the population.1 Within each sampled household, the adult
with the next birthday is selected to participate. Other details of the ABS recruitment protocol
have changed over time but are available upon request.2 Prior to 2018, the ATP was recruited
using landline and cellphone random-digit-dial surveys administered in English and Spanish.

A national sample of U.S. adults has been recruited to the ATP approximately once per year
since 2014. In some years, the recruitment has included additional efforts (known as an

Sample design
The overall target population for this survey was noninstitutionalized persons ages 18 and older
living in the United States. It featured a stratified random sample from the ATP in which the
following groups were oversampled: Hispanic adults, non-Hispanic Asian adults, and 18- to 29-
year-olds. The remaining panelists were sampled at rates designed to ensure that the share of
respondents in each stratum is proportional to its share of the U.S. adult population to the
greatest extent possible. Respondent weights are adjusted to account for differential
probabilities of selection as described in the Weighting section below.

Questionnaire development and testing
The questionnaire was developed by Pew Research Center in consultation with SSRS. The web
program used for online respondents was rigorously tested on both PC and mobile devices by
the SSRS project team and Pew Research Center researchers. The SSRS project team also
populated test data that was analyzed in SPSS to ensure the logic and randomizations were
working as intended before launching the survey.

Incentives
All respondents were offered a post-paid incentive for their participation. Respondents could
choose to receive the post-paid incentive in the form of a check or gift code to Amazon.com,
Target.com or Walmart.com. Incentive amounts ranged from $5 to $20 depending on whether
the respondent belongs to a part of the population that is harder or easier to reach. Differential
incentive amounts were designed to increase panel survey participation among groups that
traditionally have low survey response propensities.

Data quality checks

To ensure high-quality data, Center researchers performed data quality checks to identify any
respondents showing patterns of satisficing. This includes checking for whether respondents left
questions blank at very high rates or always selected the first or last answer presented. As a
result of this checking, eight ATP respondents were removed from the survey dataset prior to
weighting and analysis.



Use of artificial intelligence (AI)

Survey responses to some open-ended questions were initially coded using AI-assisted text

generated codes prior to analysis.
